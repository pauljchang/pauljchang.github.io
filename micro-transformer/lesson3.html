How does the model know which earlier word matters?

Lesson 2's slots worked, but they were rigid: every slot had its own fixed weights, so the model needed a maximum sentence length decided in advance. This lesson's sentences also come in different shapes, and the word that decides the answer moves around. In hunter sleeps in ___ the answer (cave) depends on hunter, two words back; wolves sleep in the forest. In hunter drinks ___ the answer is water no matter who's drinking. The model needs a way to look back at whichever word matters this time.

That's attention. The current word asks a question, its query. Every word so far, including the current one, offers a key (what it can answer) and a value (what it passes along if chosen). The better a key matches the query, the more weight that word gets; the weights add up to 100%, and the model takes that weighted mix of the values. One more piece real transformers use: the mix is added to the current word's own vector (a residual connection), so looking back at "hunter" never makes the model forget it's standing on "in."

The same three 16×16 matrices are used at every position, so a longer sentence doesn't need more of them; only the number of keys to compare against grows. The position vectors are still a fixed table, though (5 rows here), which is why real models still have a maximum context length. Is this the whole story on real attention? ▾ It's the real mechanism at the smallest honest scale. Production transformers add two things. Multiple heads: several independent query/key/value sets running side by side, so the model can track several kinds of relationship at once. Stacked layers: the output of one attention layer feeds the next, so relationships can build on each other. This lesson uses one head and one layer: enough to see routing happen, not enough to see everything attention can do.

Where is the model looking?

Predict first: to finish hunter sleeps in ___, which word should the model look at most? And for hunter drinks ___?
For sleeps in, the subject: cave or forest depends on who's sleeping. For drinks, the subject barely matters, since every animal here drinks water. Click "Train 500" and compare the two rows. In most runs (about 9 in 10) the model ends up putting more weight on the subject when the subject decides the answer. Nobody tells it to; it discovers that from next-word prediction alone. Small models sometimes get stuck: if a top guess is still wrong, train more or re-randomize.

Brighter word = more attention. The outlined word is the one asking the question. Percentages always add up to 100%. A ✓ marks words that really do come next in the training data. Treat the weights as a strong hint about where the model looks, not a complete explanation: a word with little weight can still tip the answer if what it passes along is strong.
