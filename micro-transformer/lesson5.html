How does a model write a whole answer?

Every model so far guessed one next word. A chatbot answers with whole sentences, and it does that with nothing more than the same one-word guess, run in a loop: guess the next word, add it to the end, then guess again from the longer text, until it writes a period. Your question and the model's own words so far are all just input for the next guess. This is called autoregressive generation, and it's exactly how ChatGPT and Claude write.

This page's model was trained ahead of time with the same code as Lesson 4, on all the Stage 4 sentences and questions, so it's ready to answer. That's how real chatbots work too: training happens once, then the trained model answers questions. It's also a bit bigger than Lesson 4's model, using 32 numbers per word instead of 16. Why did it need to be bigger? ▾ Every answer to a "what" question repeats words from the question: bear eats what ? is answered bear eats fish . To do that, the model has to look back four or five words and copy the right one, which is a job for attention. In our test runs, a model with 16 numbers per word got only 5 of 45 "what" questions right; with 32 it gets 44 of 45. Bigger models can learn more complicated jobs; that's the scaling story in one example.

2 · Ask the same question ten times

Predict first: ask hunter eats what ? ten times. With "Top word," how many different answers do you get? With "Random draw"?
Top word always gives the same answer: same input, same probabilities, same top choice. Random draw picks in proportion to the probabilities, so you get several of the four true answers (meat, fish, deer, berry). That's why a chatbot can answer the same question differently each time. Temperature controls how adventurous the draw is: low sticks to the likeliest words, high gives unlikely words a real chance. In our tests, about 95% of answers were true facts at temperature 1, but only about 60% at 2.

3 · Mistakes compound

A model never goes back and fixes a word it has written. Each word becomes part of the input for every word after it. So if one early word is wrong, the rest of the answer is built on top of the mistake. Below, we force a wrong first word, then let the model finish with its top choices.

Predict first: if the answer to hunter is what ? is forced to start with deer, what does the model write next?
It carries on smoothly: deer is hungry . That's grammatical, confident, and false in this world (deer is not hungry). The model doesn't notice that it has stopped answering the question. It just predicts the likeliest next words for the text in front of it, including its own mistake.

You can do the same in section 1: click any word in the list to choose it yourself, then let the model continue.

Why this matters for real chatbots. A large language model's context is everything it can see when choosing the next word: your conversation plus everything it has written so far. Its own earlier words stay in that context and shape everything after them. That's one reason errors snowball: once a model has stated something false, it tends to stay consistent with it, even inventing further false details to support it (Zhang et al., 2023, "How Language Model Hallucinations Can Snowball"). Part of the cause is that models are trained on correct text, where every earlier word is right, so they get little practice recovering from their own mistakes.

Ways around it: a lower temperature makes a bad early pick less likely; grounding answers in looked-up facts gives the model correct text to build on; and letting a model write out its reasoning means a later step can notice an earlier one is wrong and say so, which is the topic of Lesson 6.
