Did it learn, or just memorize?

Every lesson so far has trained a model and checked it on the same sentences it trained on. That proves very little: a model that memorized every sentence would pass. The real test is something it has never seen. So this lesson hides a few facts before training starts, trains on everything else, and then asks about the hidden ones.

First, what "training" measures. After each word the model gives every possible next word a probability. The loss is how surprised it was by the word that actually came next: −log(probability it gave the right word). Each training step nudges every weight a little in the direction that lowers the average loss over all the training sentences. That's gradient descent, and it's the whole of training.

Same model as Lesson 3 (one attention head), now with more facts, including false ones stated with "not": deer hunts not wolf . Why can't loss reach zero here? ▾ Because some sentences genuinely have more than one right ending. "hunter eats" is followed by meat, fish, deer and berry in the training data, so the best any model can do is split its guess four ways, and the loss on that word can't go below about 1.4. Real language is full of this kind of ambiguity, which is why real models never reach zero loss either.

1 · Two loss curves

training sentences hidden facts (never trained on)
Predict first: training loss will go down, since that's what training does. What will the hidden-fact loss do if you keep training to 1,000 steps or more?
It drops fast at first, then after about 250 steps it starts creeping back up while training loss keeps falling. In all 10 runs we tried, it was higher at step 1,000 than at step 250. From then on the model is getting better at its training sentences by memorizing their specifics, and getting more confidently wrong about what it hasn't seen. This is overfitting. Real labs track a held-out loss like this one and stop training, or add more data, when it starts climbing.

2 · Can it guess a hidden fact?

For each hidden fact, we take every noun and adjective the model never saw after those words in training, and ask where the hidden answer ranks among them. Rank #1 means it's the model's top guess for something new.

Predict first: which of these six can the model guess, and why those?
The four pattern facts. Bear acts like wolf, hunter and dog, which are all hungry; dog acts like the other brave predators; deer fears every other predator. They ranked in the top 3 of about 25 new words in every run we tried. The two one-offs have no pattern to borrow from: nothing else here has a spear or lives in the river. Spear sometimes sneaks into the top few anyway, just because it's one of the few nouns that ever follows a verb, but river lands near the bottom every time. Learning a pattern from similar examples is what generalization means.

3 · Does "not" carry over?

These six questions were hidden, but their answer was in the training data in another form. The model trained on deer is not brave . Does it answer deer is brave ? with no?

Predict first: will the model use the "not" sentences it trained on to answer these?
Mostly no. In our tests it answered roughly 7 in 10 of the "no" questions wrongly with yes, and it did about as badly in a test where it never saw the "not" sentences at all. To the model, "deer is not brave ." and "deer is brave ? no ." are unrelated word sequences, and for questions it hasn't seen it falls back on yes, the most common answer. The two "yes" rows look right, but only because it says yes to almost everything new.

Large models show a version of this too, called the reversal curse: a model trained on "A is B" often can't answer "B is A" (Berglund et al., 2023). Knowledge stored in one wording doesn't automatically carry over to another.
